Pacific Crest Trail
===================

.. figure:: /_static/img/pct-whitney.jpg
   :alt: Panorama from the summit of Mount Whitney at sunrise, granite peaks lit orange

   Sunrise from the top of Mount Whitney, 14,505 feet, in 2013.

In 2013 I quit my job to walk the Pacific Crest Trail,
2,650 miles from Mexico to Canada through California, Oregon, and Washington.
I started at the border in Campo on April 15
and walked about 800 miles,
through the desert and into the High Sierra,
until a stress fracture in my left foot ended the walk at Kearsarge Pass in mid-June.

It didn't go the whole way, but it changed what I wanted to do with my life.

Leaving
-------

The plan was five months on the trail, April to the end of September.
I left Urban Airship in January to train for it,
and wrote about that decision in :doc:`A Walk in the Woods </blog/2013/jan/10/walk-woods>`.

Training was simple:
walk ten miles a day with a thousand feet of climbing,
and carry 20 to 30 pounds twice a week.
The longest day was 20 miles from Terwilliger through the west hills into Forest Park.
In March I summited Mount St. Helens as a training hike.
:doc:`Prepping for the Pacific Crest Trail </blog/2013/apr/4/preping-pct>` covers the training, the gear, and the nerves:

   The things that scare you the most are the most important to do.

On the trail
------------

I flew to San Diego on April 14 and started walking the next morning.
I wrote this in Idyllwild, around mile 150, ten days in:

   The trail has been an amazing mental experience as well. I've run into some amazing people, and it's only been about 10 days. I've hiked with the same group (Team Horseshoe) until now, and have leap-frogged a few other groups (Team America, Skip & 2Step, Legend, Double Sprainbow & Ollie, Mike & Jill). It feels like I've known these folks for years, and it's been less than 2 weeks. The trail builds an amazing kind of rapport, where you can let your guard down and get to know people well. The removal of "What do you do?" from the common vocabulary of life is a welcome one. It feels like you get farther into the interesting tidbits of life faster. The small talk becomes deeper.

   I've heard about a study that says people are more creative in rooms with taller ceilings. Out here it feels like there is no ceiling, and along with that no limit to how one might think about the world. It's neat seeing what creative ways people have of eating, sleeping, walking. All of these things we take for granted in everyday life become all there is, and each person has a different take on it.

   Under the stars at night, the mind is free to wonder. There are no deadlines, no bosses, and no force to the way of life. Everyone is here because they want to be, kindred spirits from across the globe. They come together for the most fruitless and senseless act, to walk, yet it means so much at the same time. The act of removing yourself from society ("the other world") is a strong statement, while, also being a very easy one. So much about walking this path feels right, much more than walking down a street ever has.

   So it continues, only 10 days in, the great migration along the path of most resistance. As a great man once said, the best adventures answer questions that you didn't even know to ask.

It's amazing how fully formed those thoughts were so early on.
The two months that followed were the best of my life:
the desert, the first big climbs of the Sierra,
and sunrise from the top of Mount Whitney.

Coming home
-----------

The stress fracture was in the third metatarsal of my left foot.
I walked out over Kearsarge Pass and came home to six weeks in a boot.
Re-entering normal life was harder than I expected,
and not finishing was harder still, because I had never thought about quitting.

That summer I wrote to an old friend,
in a letter I later posted as :doc:`Letter to an old friend </blog/2013/sep/28/letter-to-an-old-friend>`:

   The months I spent on the trail were some of the happiest of my life.
   I will look to hopefully relive them again in a few years,
   but like many things in life, the first time is likely to be the sweetest.

While the foot healed I took up bike touring, so I could keep exploring.
That fall I also decided to work on documentation full time.
The :doc:`2013 year in review </blog/2013/dec/31/2013-year-review>` tells the whole year.

Back on the trail
-----------------

I have kept going back to pieces of it:

* **2019**: a week on the PCT in Washington, near Lake Chelan, six years after the first walk.
* **2021**: a week-long loop from Snoqualmie Pass that followed the PCT.
* **2026**: the John Muir Trail, northbound from Cottonwood Pass,
  back through the High Sierra where the 2013 walk ended.

Every trip since is on the :doc:`Adventures </adventures>` page, with a map.
